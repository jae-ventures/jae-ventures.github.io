## You Know Your City Needs Public Charging. Now What?

If you're reading this it's likely you know your city needs EV charging today or will sooner or later. You understand that the EV adoption is accelerating and that your city should prepare for the influx of battery electric vehicles (BEVs) on the road that will require proper infrastructure for the daily commuters, business fleets, or travelers passing through.

Even if the majority of charging is done at home today, forward thinking cities recognize that there are community members who need charging beyond the home. Renters in apartments or even single-family homes need access to convenient Level 2 charging, whether on-site or at the places they park at regularly. Landlords may not be ready financially or EV literate enough to understand the needs of current residents desiring to go electric or prepare for future residents that are already plugged-in. Local business fleets may be in similar situations where they lack the EV know how to install the right EVSE at their fleet parking lots. For an business or location who could benefit from EV charging on-site, there's a need to better understand the lifecycle cost savings that come with going electric without diminishing their business operations but rather supercharging them.

City leaders and community enthusiasts alike have the power to shift the narrative and prove that we can be ready for electrification today. By making the first move and establishing actionable plans and programs to invest in public EVSE, they can build a robust and widely available charging network that helps citizens see electrification is here and ready for them today. With that said, let's venture into how to electrify your city through ubiquitous EV charging opportunities.

## Get Started and Stay Committed

The first step cities can make is to simply *get started!* Bring your enthusiasm and be committed to the ongoing process of preparing your city for adopting electric vehicles. No matter what happens along the way, understand that these changes have to happen to keep people mobile in this new electric era. This will require adaptation as you learn lessons of what works and doesn't for your city. This is okay. It's a part of the process. Take in the *feedback from community members*, *work closely with your EVSE providers* and partners, and *go electric yourself!* To be a real champion of electric vehicle adoption in your city, you need to understand what being an EV driver is like on a daily basis. If you find it not feasible to trade-in for an EV or purchase one, rent one when you go on trips or find a local or friend who would love to share their experience with you regularly. The idea is to stay in tune with the lived experience that you will be shaping with your community.

> - Get feedback from the community
> - Work closely with your EV infrastructure providers/partners
> - Drive electric yourself!

This is a process better done with others. Learn from other experts or leaders from similar cities who have successfully introduced EV charging infrastructure to their communities. Work with organizations and non-profits like teh [Electrification Coalition](https://electrificationcoalition.org/), [Veloz](https://www.veloz.org/), or [Forth Mobility](https://forthmobility.org/) who help cities make a plan for building robust and accessible charging networks in their cities and develop plans for a successful EV infrastructure rollout. You will likely have something to teach them to and will foster a feeling of growth and excitement as more and more places go electric.

## Developing a Robust Plan

A major hurdle for many cities is building a sustainable plan that starts off with low cost and risk, but grows with the EV demand in their city. An iterative approach would be best to get started quickly yet still provide value. Find the public places where people  already gather regularly. Town centers, shopping plazas, parks, and other community centric locations are great places to start. From there you start small by installing a few well placed and reliable [Level 2 chargers](https://www.transportation.gov/rural/ev/toolkit/ev-basics/charging-speeds) (capable of delivering 10 to 30 miles in 30 minutes) in these locations to keep cost low but still show commitment to EV drivers.

I recommend Level 2 charging first versus shiny and expensive DC Fast charging because it not only reduces the initial cost of electrifying your city, but when done well integrates easily with people's daily behaviors and can be a low cost option for drivers who can't afford the same hardware at home or live in an apartment. People already go to the gym for 30 minutes to a couple hours 3 times a week or more. They go on a grocery haul weekly or bi-weekly. They go to work for 8 hours at a time nearly every day. Those are charging opportunities that at the least will cover their daily commute and at most will have them only see a DC Fast charger when they travel out of town. This benefits not only the drivers who can't charge at home when they sleep, but also the businesses that now offer a new commodity that gets them additional foot traffic.

To keep costs realistic an iterative phased approach that grows with your city's demand can be done in phases. **Phase 1** starts with Level 2 charging to show the public that charging is present in the places where they shop, eat, work, and play. This shows citizens that its possible to start driving electric. **Phase 2** is where you will evaluate the experience of EV drivers (which you are now!) and determine if you just need more Level 2 stations in the right locations or can add DC Fast charging near transit corridors and places where people dwell for shorter amounts of time like 15 minutes or so. Of course this approach is adaptable, but the core idea is to do something small that works well and is reliable, and build more as your demand builds.

> **Phase 1:** Install Level 2 Charging in busy areas where people stay for 1-2 hours
> **Phase 2:** Evaluate EV charging experience and plan to expand more Level 2 or install first DC Fast Charging sites

## Getting the Community Charged Up

Keeping the community engaged with your project will be important in making this transition to electrified transport. Engagement will help community members feel like they are a part of the change rather than on the other side of it and being forced into it.

Here are some activities that can educate people on what EV charging infrastructure in their city actually means for them.

### EV Ride-Alongs

Introduce people to what the EV lifestyle is like by taking them on a tour of the new EV Charging locations in the city in an EV. Give them exposure to what the driving experience is like and what charging an EV at the places they likely already frequent is like. You can remove the mystery of that "shiny new thing in the parking lot" and open up their mind to the ease and convenience of going electric.

### EV Charging Meet-Ups

Get EV Drivers connected with each other through EV Charging meet ups, where they can recharge their cars and themselves. These can be like *Cars and Coffee* events where EV enthusiasts and new drivers alike can talk about their experiences. Not only does this creates exposure for EVs and your new charging stations for those passing by, it's a great opportunity to get raw feedback real EV drivers on the infrastructure you've installed so far. Champion someone or an organization like your utility to hold these regularly to continue gauging driver feedback.

### EV Educational Pop-Ups and Events

Organize your team of EV infrastructure advocates to host pop-up events and show up as sponsor of other events where there is an opportunity to teach people about EVs and EV infrastructure. Showing up at Science Fairs, Back-to-School events, Earth days, or wherever your team feels appropriate can help you gain additional exposure throughout the year and inform the public how easy it is to own and drive an EV in your city, whether they own a home or not.

## Looking Toward Successfully Electrified Cities

Here are some cities, regions, and states that are melting away range anxiety for many EV drivers through their electrification efforts. Let their EV infrastructure plans be an inspiration for your own.

### Fort Collins, Colorado

Fort Collins got it right by taking a comprehensive, coordinated approach to EV readiness. In 2018, they developed a detailed [Electric Vehicle Readiness Roadmap](https://www.fcgov.com/fcmoves/files/cofc-ev-readiness-roadmap.pdf?1540496524) that established clear vision, goals, and defined roles for City departments, private sector, and community members. Rather than having different departments work in silos, they assigned FC Moves as the lead department while establishing an interdepartmental EV working group that meets twice yearly for ongoing coordination.

What really set Fort Collins apart was their commitment to leading by example. As of 2024, they've added 40 EVs and 3 full-sized electric transit buses to their municipal fleet while deploying over 50 EV charging stations throughout the city. This wasn't just about infrastructure—they updated their building codes to require EV-ready single-family homes with conduit from electrical panels to garages prepared for 30A, 240V outlets for Level 2 charging, making it easier for residents to go electric.

Their strategic approach paid off when they secured over $1 million in grants for cutting-edge EV charging management solutions. As highlighted by the [Build America Center](https://bac.umd.edu/fort-collins-smart-grid-electric-vehicle-charge-management-solution/), their success came from emphasizing collaboration, obtaining letters of support, and outlining a clear innovative vision. Fort Collins participates in statewide collaboration efforts like [Drive Clean Colorado](https://drivecleancolorado.org/), a non-profit coalition that works with diverse stakeholders including fleets, utilities, vehicle technology companies, and local governments to accelerate clean transportation adoption across the state.


### Northern New Jersey

The North Jersey Transportation Planning Authority (NJTPA) took a methodical, regional approach that other areas can learn from. They partnered with three pilot municipalities—Montclair Township, Town of Secaucus, and Woodbridge Township—to develop comprehensive local readiness plans, then created a detailed [Alternative Fuel Vehicle Readiness Guidebook](https://www.njtpa.org/NJTPA/media/Documents/Planning/Regional-Programs/Alternative-Fuel-Vehicles/NJTPA-AFV-Readiness-Guidebook_Dec2017_FINAL(1).pdf) that any municipality can use.

What made their approach effective was the emphasis on stakeholder engagement from day one. They formed diverse committees including local government officials, fleet managers, utilities, businesses, and community organizations, ensuring that everyone had a voice in the planning process. Rather than focusing on just one type of vehicle, they addressed multiple alternative fuel types including plug-in electric vehicles, natural gas vehicles, propane, biodiesel, ethanol, and hydrogen.

The NJTPA region also tackled one of the biggest barriers to EV adoption: charging at multi-unit dwellings. They specifically addressed the challenges renters face and included strategies for serving low-income communities, recognizing that electrification needs to work for everyone, not just homeowners. Their regulatory framework was solid too—they developed clear definitions for EVs and charging infrastructure, updated zoning codes, and streamlined permitting processes to make it easier for businesses and residents to install charging stations.

### North Carolina

North Carolina approached electrification as an economic development opportunity, and it's paying off big time. They've attracted $20.4 billion in private investments in the EV manufacturing industry, creating 16,300 jobs in the EV sector. As detailed by the [Electrification Coalition's North Carolina EV Policy overview](https://electrificationcoalition.org/work/state-ev-policy/north-carolina-ev-policy/), this wasn't accidental—it was the result of strategic planning and supportive policies.

Their corridor strategy is particularly smart. North Carolina secured $109 million over five years for highway charging corridors, with plans to install fast, reliable charging every 50 miles along almost 4,000 miles of alternative fuel corridors. All charging sites will be located within a mile of highways, making it convenient for travelers passing through the state.

North Carolina also understood that fleet electrification could be a powerful demonstration tool. They've received 145 electric school buses through the Clean School Bus program across multiple districts, showing students and families that electric vehicles are reliable and practical for daily use. They didn't do this alone—28 business leaders signed a letter urging acceleration of medium- and heavy-duty vehicle electrification, showing strong private sector support.

What's particularly impressive is their focus on rural communities. Through targeted roundtables and community engagement in partnership with other south eastern states through the [Southern Alliance for Clean Energy](https://cleanenergy.org/), they've held webinars like the [Virtual Roundtable: "Transportation Electrification in the Southeast" Fifth Annual Report](https://cleanenergy.org/resource/virtual-roundtable-transportation-electrification-in-the-southeast-fifth-annual-report/) where they've worked to ensure that rural areas aren't left behind in the transition to electric transportation. By 2023, North Carolina had topped 70,000 electric vehicle registrations, proving that their comprehensive approach is working.

## Venturing Further: What's Next for Electrified Cities?

Throughout this exploration, we've seen that successful city electrification isn't just about installing a few charging stations and hoping for the best. It requires understanding that EV infrastructure is essential for meeting the needs of current and future residents who can't charge at home, getting started with committed leadership and community engagement, following a thoughtful process that builds from small wins to comprehensive networks, and learning from cities like Fort Collins, Northern New Jersey, and North Carolina that have shown what's possible through strategic planning, strong partnerships, and leading by example.

But let's take a step back and imagine what your city could look like when electrification truly takes hold. While it may be like the AI generated picture below with lots of fancy cars and beautiful city streets, its important to important to envision the value added to people's lives as well.

Imagine walking down Main Street and taking a deep breath of cleaner air that doesn't irritate your nose or trigger your kid's asthma. It's about parents saving hundreds of dollars each month on transportation costs because electric vehicles have such low operating expenses, money that can go toward groceries, education, or that next family trip. It's about local businesses thriving because they've become destinations where people charge their cars while grabbing coffee, shopping, or meeting friends.

This electrified future is about building community in ways we haven't seen before. EV charging stations become gathering places, new third spaces where strangers turned neighbors meet while their cars power up. Local jobs are created installing, maintaining, and managing charging networks. Tourism increases because your city becomes known as a welcoming, forward-thinking place that makes it easy for electric vehicle travelers to visit and explore.

Most importantly, it's about laying the foundation for lasting growth that benefits everyone in your community. When you make it easy for people to go electric, you're not just reducing emissions—you're showing that your city is ready for the future, attracting new residents and businesses that want to be part of something positive and sustainable.

The cities that get started today, that begin with those first few Level 2 charging hubs in the right locations, that engage their communities and plan strategically, will be the ones leading this transformation. The question isn't whether electrification will happen in your city—it's whether you'll be ready to guide the process in a way that benefits everyone.

Your electrified city is waiting to be built. The only question is: are you ready to venture into making it a reality?
